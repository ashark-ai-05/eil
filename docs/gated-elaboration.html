Requirements an AI can't fake

An AI turns a ticket into requirements. Then a program — not another AI — reads what it wrote and refuses it if anything was invented, left undecided, or signed off by the AI itself.

01 — the thing itself

One paragraph in. Testable requirements out.

A real ticket: Risk Ops want to change a counterparty's credit limit during the trading day, without restarting anything. One paragraph of background, and no mention of what should happen when things go wrong. Here is one of the six requirements that came back.

requirement 6 of 6testable

A credit check that cannot be evaluated during an amendment rejects the order.

Given a gateway that cannot evaluate the credit check
When an order arrives for that counterparty
Then the order is rejected with a credit-unavailable reason code
And the rejection records which control could not be evaluated

That is the shape of every one of them. Something a tester can run, with a stakeholder, a trigger and an observable outcome — not a paragraph of intent that three people read three ways.

02 — why it needs checking

The same ticket, without the checker

An AI given that ticket and left alone writes something that reads just as well. It also quietly decides things nobody asked it to decide.

no checkerinvented

“If the limit service is unavailable, the order is allowed to proceed so trading is not interrupted.”

with the checkergrounded

“A check that cannot be evaluated rejects the order, and records which control could not be evaluated.”

Nobody told it either answer. It picked one, wrote it in the voice of someone who knew, and moved on. That was one of nine such decisions in a single document — a latency figure, who may approve an amendment, whether exposure nets, what happens to orders already at the venue. Each is a real decision with a right answer, and none of them were flagged as decisions at all.

03 — how it works

The AI estimates. A program decides. A person signs.

The ticket
One paragraph, as written by the person who wanted it.
The AI writes
It may estimate how unclear and how hard each part is. Nothing else it says is taken on trust.
The program checks
46 specific problems it knows how to look for. No AI anywhere in this step.
People sign
Three named people. The document cannot mark itself approved.

Why the third box has no AI in it. An AI that grades its own homework will pass it. The checker is ordinary code with a fixed list of problems, so the same document gets the same verdict every time, and you can read the code that made the decision.

04 — six ways to cheat, and what happens

Each row is one field changed by hand in an approved document, and what the checker printed when it was run again. Same edit, same answer, every time.

Someone editsOne word inside a quotation from the wiki — “aiming” becomes “elephant”

Someone editsThe approval, from a named person to the AI itself
“approver "d.mercer" for role "PO" signed as kind "agent"; expected "human" — an agent cannot sign off its own requirements” refused · gate-006
Someone editsEmpties the index linking every test back to the requirement it proves
the index is rebuilt from the document, so an edited one disagrees with what it indexes — and six tests are now untraceable refused · meta-002 · trace-001
The fourth one is the one to remember: a quotation cannot be invented, because the checker goes back to the page and looks for the words. The fifth is the line the whole thing exists to draw.

05 — when it doesn't know

It looks it up. If it can't, it asks — and stops.

Before anyone is interrupted, it searches what the organisation has already written down: our own docs first, then the wiki and the ticket system. A person is the last resort, not the first.

found itthe source hedged

How quickly does an approved limit change reach the gateways?

“Was aiming for 250ms end to end from the change landing in credit-admin to the gateway acting on it, and I think we got roughly there, but I haven't measured recently.”

From Gateway Notes — an unlabelled page, last edited fourteen months ago by someone who has since left. Nobody was going to find it. The check took about forty milliseconds.

The author hedged, so the answer was not written down as a fact. It is carried as a known risk that s.iyer accepted by name, with a note to measure it before build starts.

couldn't find itasked a person

What happens to orders already working at the venue when a limit is cut?

Nothing anywhere says. So it did not guess — it put the question in the document with d.mercer's name against it, and the document cannot be signed until it is answered.

The closest thing it found was PTR-420, a ticket in our own Jira asking that exact question. Still open. Nobody has decided. It was found in twenty minutes rather than in testing.

That last case is the hard one. A search engine would have called PTR-420 a match — it is entirely about the question. It just doesn't answer it. Being about something and answering it are different, and only one of them is safe to build on.

What this is not, yet. This covers requirements only — design, planning and implementation are specified and not built. The wiki pages and tickets in this run are made up for the demonstration and each says so on its face; they exist so the whole thing runs on a laptop with nothing installed and no network. The AI's answers here are replayed from a recorded run so it comes out the same every time; everything that checks them runs live. We record how often the AI is called and how long it takes — not what it costs.
